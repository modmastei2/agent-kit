# Composition

Composition is how the masses, openings, and details of a structure relate to each other
as a whole — the difference between "shapes near each other" and "a designed building."

## Symmetry and asymmetry

- **Symmetric** composition reads as formal, civic, ceremonial, or authoritative. Use it
  deliberately for structures that need to project order (town halls, temples, palace
  gates) — not as a default because it's easier to build.
- **Controlled asymmetry** balances masses of different size/weight around an implied
  center rather than mirroring them. A large primary mass can be balanced by a smaller but
  more detailed or more vertical secondary mass. This is usually what "asymmetric but not
  chaotic" means.
- **Uncontrolled asymmetry** has no balancing logic at all — masses were added without
  regard for how the composition reads as a whole. This is a critique-checklist failure,
  not a style choice.

Symmetry preference is a taste dimension (`taste_profile.symmetry.preference`) — don't
assume full symmetry or full asymmetry; ask or infer.

## Hierarchy

Every composition needs a clear order of visual importance: primary mass first, secondary
masses second, attachments and details last. If every element competes for attention
equally, the composition has no hierarchy and reads as noise even if each part is well
detailed individually.

## Focal points

A composition benefits from one dominant focal point (a tower, a grand entrance, a gable)
rather than several competing ones. Secondary focal points should be subordinate in scale
or detail density to the primary one.

## Balance

Balance is not symmetry — it's the sense that visual weight (mass, height, detail density,
material darkness/lightness) is distributed in a way that doesn't feel like it's about to
tip over. A tall narrow tower on one side can be balanced by a wide low wing on the other
without either being a mirror of the other.

## What counts as a structurally distinct variation

When producing multiple directions for the same brief, vary the composition strategy
itself, not just surface choices:

- **Direction A**: dominant single vertical landmark with subordinate low wings.
- **Direction B**: wide, low, horizontally organized composition with repeated bays.
- **Direction C**: organic, clustered composition of several comparable-scale volumes.

Two directions that share the same massing and differ only in roof shape or block palette
are not distinct directions — they're one direction with two finishes.

## Building composition vs. presentation composition

Everything above governs how a structure's own masses relate to each other. A Hero
Visualization (see [hero-visualization.md](hero-visualization.md)) adds a second,
independent layer of composition: how the *scene around the structure* is staged for a
viewer. Both need to be worked out, and neither substitutes for the other — a structure
with weak building composition doesn't get fixed by good scene staging, and a strong
structure can still be presented in a flat, unstaged scene.

Presentation composition tools, once the building composition is already sound:

- **Foreground / midground / background** — what sits in front of the structure (a path,
  props, a low wall), what the structure itself occupies (mid-ground, usually the focal
  layer), and what reads behind it (sky, terrain, other structures) to give the scene
  depth beyond the building's own silhouette.
- **Entry framing** — using foreground elements (gateposts, an approach path, flanking
  props) to direct the eye toward the entrance the same way the building's own hierarchy
  already does (see "Hierarchy" and "Focal points" above) — presentation reinforcing
  architecture, not competing with it.
- **Settlement context** — whether the structure is shown isolated or amid other
  buildings/activity; this is a staging decision for the hero image, independent of
  whether the canonical design itself assumes a settlement (see SKILL.md's "Understand
  intent").
- **Faction/identity markers** — banners, heraldry, color language placed in the scene to
  reinforce who/what the structure belongs to — Scene Support or Amplification-tier
  elements (see [massing.md](massing.md)), never load-bearing to the architecture itself.
- **Scene-support masses** — non-architectural elements (crates, carts, work areas, camp
  props) staged around the structure for environmental storytelling — see
  [massing.md](massing.md)'s Scene Support Mass tier.
- **Environmental storytelling** — what the staged scene implies about use, history, or
  activity (a busy forge yard vs. an abandoned one) without changing the canonical
  architecture at all.

A design should be able to answer both questions independently: *how does the building
itself compose* (this file's main sections), and, if a hero image is being made, *how
should the scene around it be composed* (this section). See
[hero-visualization.md](hero-visualization.md) for the full workflow this feeds into.
