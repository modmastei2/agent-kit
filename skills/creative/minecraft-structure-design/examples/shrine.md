# Example: Shrine

Demonstrates a Contextual Preference (dramatic verticality) being applied appropriately —
see references/architectural-styles.md and taste/taste-profile-schema.md on Contextual
Preferences.

## Intent
- Type: shrine
- Style: Japanese-influenced (user-specified)
- Purpose: quiet devotional structure, not a settlement centerpiece
- Scale: small, but the brief calls for it to feel taller than its footprint suggests

## Design thesis
A single-bay shrine beneath a tiered dark-wood roof disproportionately tall for its
small footprint, reached by a straight stone approach that frames the building as a
destination rather than a passing structure.

## LOD1–2
- Primary mass: a small enclosed shrine chamber, modest footprint.
- Silhouette: a two-tier roof whose combined height clearly exceeds the wall height below
  it — an intentional proportion exaggeration (references/proportions.md) applied here
  because "dramatic verticality on temples/shrines" is exactly the Contextual Preference
  condition it's meant for; it would not be applied to, say, a well house of the same
  size and style.
- Structural language: exposed dark wooden support brackets under each roof tier's
  overhang — explains why the tiers can project as far as they do (avoids the
  "unsupported upper volume" failure).
- Terrain integration: raised stone platform beneath the shrine, plus a straight
  approach path with a single threshold marker (torii-equivalent) — the approach is part
  of the design, not an afterthought (references/terrain-integration.md).
- Palette: stone platform, dark timber structural frame, white/light infill walls, dark
  tiled roof — four materials, clearly role-assigned, no accent needed given the small
  scale.

## Taste review note
If this user's Taste Profile generally favors low verticality and wide massing (as seen
on their houses and civic buildings), this shrine is a deliberate, labeled deviation — the
Contextual Preference for verticality on devotional structures overrides the general Soft
Preference here, and that override is stated explicitly rather than silently applied.
